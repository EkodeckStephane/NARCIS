\begin{abstract}
Selection-based coverless communication avoids pixel modification, but unchanged carriers alone do not guarantee reliable or statistically inconspicuous communication. A finite shared image index can become infeasible for a protected message after channel qualification, and repeated secret-dependent selection can itself create a detectable distribution shift. We present NARCIS, an authenticated neural coverless image-signaling protocol that treats these two effects as protocol constraints rather than post-hoc diagnostics. NARCIS combines a learned normalized image descriptor, calibration-locked quantile codebooks, complementary five-cover group banks, an exact session-balanced keyed Gray mapping, AES-GCM authenticated framing, replay control, and Reed--Solomon correction. The final operating point ($K=8$, group size 5, 128 Reed--Solomon parity bytes) was fixed on a development corpus before external evaluation on five Caltech-101 partitions. Across twelve calibration transformations, NARCIS recovered 1,800/1,800 authenticated messages. On eight unseen holdout transformations it recovered 1,163/1,200 (96.92\%); all 37 failures occurred under a 12\% central crop, while the other seven holdouts were 150/150 each. A repeated image-disjoint detector audit yielded mean macro-AUC 0.504 for SRM-lite/ExtraTrees, 0.513 for GLCM/logistic regression, and 0.527 for a 30-head CNN, establishing low but measurable selection leakage rather than undetectability. A frozen execution of the official DiffStega benchmark completed all 100 UniStega cases; its independently reproduced correct-recovery PSNR was 23.274 dB versus 23.290 dB reported by the original paper. Because DiffStega reconstructs secret images whereas NARCIS transmits authenticated byte strings through unchanged indexed images, family-specific metrics are reported without a cross-family superiority claim.
\end{abstract}
